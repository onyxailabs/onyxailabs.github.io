YouTube SEO Checklist: Score Your Video Before You Publish (2026)

Good YouTube SEO is a pre-publish discipline, not a post-publish hope: first confirm that people actually search for your topic, then write a title that matches how they type it, a description that answers before it sells, ten to fifteen honest tags, and a thumbnail with two or three huge words — and finally check the video again 48 hours after publishing, when one title or thumbnail change is still a normal, healthy move. This checklist is the exact order we work in.

Step 0 — Check demand before anything else

The most expensive SEO mistake happens before the video exists: making something excellent that nobody searches for. Type your intended topic into YouTube search and read the autocomplete suggestions — those phrases are real queries typed by real people. Open the top competing videos and note their view counts and ages: strong views on recent videos mean live demand; a graveyard of tiny channels means the topic may be too narrow, however good it is. Prefer topics with proven demand — beginner tutorials of well-known tools, head-to-head comparisons, "how to do X" problems — and let your title borrow the searcher's own words.

Title — the search phrase, wearing a reason to click

  • Front-load the searched phrase. The words people type should appear early, in the order they type them, so both the algorithm and the human eye match the video to the query instantly.
  • Keep it tight — around 60 characters or fewer — so it never truncates awkwardly on a phone, where most viewing happens.
  • Add one honest hook: a number, a result, a year, or a genuine question. Parentheses and a colon are cheap, effective punctuation — "How to Get a YouTube API Key: Create, Restrict, Test (2026)" is the shape our own channel uses.
  • Never promise what the video does not deliver. A misleading title buys a click and pays for it with abandoned watch time, which quietly buries the video.

Description — answer first, then structure

The first two or three lines are the only part most viewers (and much of the search machinery) will weigh, so spend them on a direct answer: what the viewer will be able to do, and the one link that matters if the video is a tutorial. Below that, structure earns its keep: chapter timestamps taken from the video's real section times (YouTube turns them into clickable chapters), a short paragraph naming what is covered, source links for factual claims, and a closing line that asks a real question to invite comments. Three hashtags at the end are enough; a wall of them reads as desperation.

Tags — ten to fifteen, honest and boring

Tags no longer carry a video, but they still help YouTube place it — especially for spellings and phrasings your title could not hold. Use ten to fifteen tags: your main phrase, its close variants ("how to set up an MCP server", "mcp server setup", "mcp tutorial"), the category terms a browser would use, and the specific tools named in the video. Skip tags for topics the video does not cover; misleading metadata works against you, and stuffing a tag field to its character limit is effort spent where it matters least.

Thumbnail — two or three huge words

  • Two or three huge words maximum (or one or two big numbers) — if it cannot be read at a glance, it cannot compete on a phone feed.
  • One focal point: the famous tool's logo, a striking result, or a face — never a collage, and never a screenshot of a dashboard, which shrinks into grey mush.
  • The 10% test: shrink the thumbnail to a tenth of its size. If the words are still readable in about two seconds, it passes. This single test catches most weak thumbnails before YouTube's audience does.
  • Thumbnail and title are one promise in two halves — the thumbnail should add the emotion or the result, not repeat the title word for word.

The finishing touches most channels skip

Add the video to the right playlist so sessions continue; set an end screen pointing to the single most logical next video; upload your captions file rather than trusting auto-captions with technical words; and after publishing, post a pinned comment that carries the benefit line, the one useful link, and an engagement question. None of these take five minutes. Together they are the difference between a video that was uploaded and a video that was published.

How we score it — the pre-publish gate

On the Onyx AI channel, no video is uploaded until its package passes a written score, because "it looked fine" proved unreliable. The gate we use is simple enough to copy: title 35 points (length in range, the searched phrase present, a hook element, formatting), description 20 (answer up front, a link, chapters, hashtags), tags 15 (enough tags, title keywords echoed honestly). A package must reach the full pre-publish mark — 70 out of 70 across those three parts — before the upload starts, and weak titles get rewritten until they earn it. Our tool OnyxIQ automates exactly this scoring with your own YouTube API key (the setup for that key is its own short guide), but a notebook and this checklist will take you most of the way.

The 48-hour check

Publishing is not the end of SEO; it is the start of the measurement. Forty-eight hours after a video goes live, compare its views and click-through against your channel's recent median. If it lags badly, make one change — a retitle or a thumbnail swap, not both — and log what you changed and when, so the next video's checklist learns from it. If it is performing, leave it alone. Iteration is a normal step in the method, not an admission of failure; panic-editing every few hours is the only real mistake available here.

Honest limits — what SEO cannot do

  • SEO cannot rescue a zero-demand topic. A perfect package on a video nobody searches for still finds nobody. Demand first, always — that is why it is Step 0.
  • Retention outranks metadata. Title and thumbnail win the click; the video itself must then hold the viewer, or YouTube stops offering the click to others.
  • There is no secret tag, trick or upload hour that substitutes for a searched topic, an honest promise and a video that delivers it. Anyone selling one is selling the feeling of progress.

Frequently asked questions

How many tags should a YouTube video have?

Ten to fifteen relevant tags is the practical range: your main search phrase, its close variants, and the specific tools or names in the video. Beyond that, extra tags add almost nothing — spend the effort on the title and thumbnail instead.

Does the description really matter for SEO?

Yes, but mostly at the top. The first two or three lines — a direct summary in the searcher's language, plus chapters built from real timestamps — do the work. A long description that repeats the title in different words adds length, not value.

When should I change a video's title or thumbnail?

At the 48-hour check, if the video clearly lags your channel's median — and only one element at a time, so you can tell what helped. Changing both at once, or changing things hourly, destroys the information you were trying to gather.
